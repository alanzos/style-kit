# Narrative

What the deck has to argue, in what order, and how each slide title carries it.

## The Most Important Principle: Optimize for Understanding

Y Combinator’s presentation-design guidance emphasizes **legibility, simplicity, and obviousness**. YC also recommends focusing on only the handful of ideas an audience should remember.

Source:

- https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck

MIT Communication Lab independently recommends that **each slide convey one point** and that the slide title express the main takeaway.

Source:

- https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/broad/commkit/slideshow/

Stanford’s presentation guidance similarly recommends **one slide, one idea** and advises presenters to use visual aids for concepts that genuinely benefit from visuals rather than filling slides with speech text.

Source:

- https://oralcommprogram.stanford.edu/presentation-and-delivery/visuals-and-powerpoint

### Practical consequence

Do not ask:

> “How can I fit all of this on the slide?”

Ask:

> “What is the one thing the investor should understand after seeing this slide?”

If there are three independent conclusions, consider three slides.

## Investor Decks Are Internal-Selling Documents

A founder is rarely selling to only one person.

In many VC processes:

1. One investor meets the founder.
2. That investor summarizes the opportunity.
3. Other partners inspect the deck or an internal investment memo.
4. The firm discusses concerns.
5. The founder may present to a partner meeting.

First Round notes that the point partner commonly prepares an investment memo for other partners before a partner meeting.

Source:

- https://review.firstround.com/heres-what-you-can-really-expect-when-pitching-your-seed-stage-startup-at-a-vc-partner-meeting/

Andreessen Horowitz similarly describes diligence materials as inputs that help investors prepare internal investment discussions and memos.

Source:

- https://a16z.com/the-insiders-guide-to-data-rooms-what-to-know-before-you-raise/

### Design implication

Your deck should be easy for an investor to **retell accurately**.

A useful test:

> If an investor forwards the deck to a partner without you, can that partner correctly explain the company, problem, market, traction, business model, and reason to believe?

## Narrative Before Slides

Techstars’ 2026-05-07 guidance argues that founders often over-focus on deck production when the real product is the story. Its recommendation is to write the narrative before designing the slides.

Source:

- https://www.techstars.com/blog/founder-advice/why-most-pitch-decks-dont-work-and-how-to-make-sure-yours-does

### Recommended workflow

1. Write the investment story in prose.
2. Reduce it to approximately 5-7 memorable claims.
3. Put the claims in a logical sequence.
4. Turn each major claim into a slide headline.
5. Add evidence that proves each headline.
6. Remove any slide that does not advance the argument.
7. Build appendix slides for predictable objections.
8. Rehearse the story.
9. Only then refine visual design.

This sequencing is consistent with YC’s emphasis on a small set of memorable ideas.

Source:

- https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck

## Recommended Seed-Deck Architecture

DocSend’s updated 2026 seed guidance recommends a deck of approximately 19 to 20 pages containing 12 core sections.

Source:

- https://www.docsend.com/blog/what-vcs-really-want-to-see-inside-your-seed-deck/

Each section exists to answer one question an investor will ask anyway. A practical sequence is:

| Order | Section | Core question |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Company purpose | What are you? |
| 2 | Problem | What important pain exists? |
| 3 | Solution | Why is your approach compelling? |
| 4 | Product | What actually exists? |
| 5 | Business model | Who pays, what do they pay, and what does it cost to serve? Then: how do you acquire customers, and how does usage become revenue and margin? |
| 6 | Market | Can this become large, and what can the company become if it succeeds? |
| 7 | Why now | Why is this opportunity newly possible or newly urgent? |
| 8 | Competition | What are the alternatives and why do you win? |
| 9 | Traction | What evidence proves demand? |
| 10 | Team | Why are you the team to build this? |
| 11 | Financials | What do the economics and runway look like? |
| 12 | Fundraising ask | What are you raising, and what will the new capital allow you to prove? |

Two positions in that table were moved from the sequence this document originally
carried, and both moves are argued in "Where the business model belongs" below.
Business model moved from 10 to 5, the position DocSend measured in decks that
raised. It now precedes Market, so the market number has units by the time the
reader meets it.

Product moved from 6 to 4 to stay next to it and in front of it. DocSend's reading
of the successful decks is that they couple product directly to monetization near
the front. Why now, Competition, Traction and Team each shifted down one and keep
their relative order, which no evidence speaks to either way.

The set of questions above is consistent with frameworks from Sequoia, Y Combinator, DocSend, NFX, Techstars, and First Round.

Sources:

- https://sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan/
- https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-raise-a-seed-round/
- https://www.docsend.com/blog/what-vcs-really-want-to-see-inside-your-seed-deck/
- https://www.nfx.com/post/the-non-obvious-guide-to-fundraising
- https://www.techstars.com/blog/founder-advice/why-most-pitch-decks-dont-work-and-how-to-make-sure-yours-does
- https://review.firstround.com/heres-what-you-can-really-expect-when-pitching-your-seed-stage-startup-at-a-vc-partner-meeting/

DocSend reports that opening with company purpose, then problem, then solution, then market is associated with stronger fundraising outcomes in its dataset.

Source:

- https://www.docsend.com/blog/what-vcs-really-want-to-see-inside-your-seed-deck/

### Important caveat

Do not follow the sequence mechanically when your company has a stronger opening.

For example:

- A startup with extraordinary traction may lead with traction.
- A famous founder may use team earlier.
- A deep-tech company may need a brief technical breakthrough slide earlier.
- A highly visual consumer product may open with the product experience.

Techstars explicitly warns against treating decks as a paint-by-numbers exercise.

### Where the business model belongs, and the one thing anybody measured

The sequence above used to put business model at 10, which is the position
DocSend associates with decks that failed. It is now at 5. This subsection is
why.

DocSend's pre-seed report covered over 200 startups from 2022 to the first half of
2023. It found the business model section at position 4 in decks that raised and
position 10 in decks that did not. Its own reading is that successful founders
couple product directly to monetization at the front of the deck. This is the only
measurement anywhere of where a section actually sits in funded versus unfunded
decks, so it deserves more weight than any template.

DocSend's two datasets disagree with each other. The 2015 study with Harvard
Business School measured against a section order with business model at 8. Time on
the business model section also flips sign between reports: 94 percent more in
*successful* decks in one cohort, 233 percent more in *unsuccessful* decks in the
next. So time on
section is not a directional signal, and only the position finding is usable.

**The published templates split, roughly four to three for monetization before
market sizing:**

| Before market | After market |
|---|---|
| Y Combinator (BM 7, market 8) | Sequoia (BM 7, market 5, competition 6) |
| 500 Global (BM 6, competition 7, market 8) | Kawasaki (BM 8, sales 6, competition 7) |
| Founder Institute (BM 6, market 7) | |
| Techstars, Brohl (BM 6, market 7) | |
| CRV (BM 5, market 6) | |

Andreessen Horowitz publishes no slide order at all: every "a16z template" in
circulation is a third-party reconstruction by a deck vendor.

**The argument that does not depend on anyone's opinion is arithmetic.** A credible
bottom-up market number is price multiplied by customers. Put the market slide
first and you are asking the reader to accept a number whose units they have not
been given yet.

That objection gets sharper the further the payer sits from the user. In a B2B2C
model, a marketplace, or a regulated product where a partner pays and someone else
is served, the market slide is unreadable until the reader knows who pays.

**Countervailing considerations.** Founder Institute states that the middle block,
roughly slides five through nine, is meant to be rearranged to fit the narrative.
Techstars writes that there is no definitive rule about order. Sequoia, the single
most copied template in the world, puts business model after both market and
competition.

At pre-seed the business model slide is a one-line monetization hypothesis, which
is cheap to place early. By Series A it is unit economics, which may need the
market established first.

**Evidence class.** No A/B test, randomized trial or controlled experiment on deck
section order exists in the public record. The largest dataset of funded versus
unfunded decks, 17,546 of them, has published nothing on order. The persuasion
literature supports putting strong material early for a high-elaboration audience
such as an investor
([Haugtvedt and Wegener 1994](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/21/1/205/1811820)),
but that is about argument order rather than deck sections. So: a defensible lean,
not a settled result.

One practical pressure points the same way. Average seed-deck first-read time has
fallen below two minutes in DocSend's 2023 cohort, and investor time per deck has
declined every year measured. The less time a reader gives you, the more expensive
it is to put anything load-bearing late.

Source:

- https://www.docsend.com/index/

Source:

- https://www.techstars.com/blog/founder-advice/why-most-pitch-decks-dont-work-and-how-to-make-sure-yours-does

## Use Assertion Headlines

MIT Communication Lab recommends titles that communicate the takeaway, not merely the subject.

Source:

- https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/broad/commkit/slideshow/

Harvard Catalyst similarly recommends beginning body slides with a strong headline.

Source:

- https://catalyst.harvard.edu/writing-communication-center/visualize-science/slides/

The same rule governs a chart’s headline, which states the chart’s conclusion rather than naming the measure; see `06-charts-and-data.md`.

### Weak to strong examples

| Weak title | Stronger title |
|---|---|
| Market | **$6.8B of annual spend is shifting to API-first platforms** |
| Problem | **Claims teams lose 11 hours per week to manual reconciliation** |
| Product | **One workflow replaces four disconnected tools** |
| Traction | **ARR grew 4.2× in twelve months while churn fell** |
| Competition | **Legacy vendors require 3× the implementation time** |
| Business model | **Expansion revenue lifts mature accounts above $40k ACV** |
| Team | **We spent 18 years building the infrastructure we are replacing** |
| Ask | **$3M gets us to enterprise repeatability and Series A readiness** |

### Headline-only test

Read only the slide titles in sequence.

If the titles do not form a coherent investment story, rewrite them.

## The Opening

Techstars’ 2026 storytelling guidance encourages founders to find an opening that creates genuine curiosity rather than beginning with generic company history.

Source:

- https://www.techstars.com/blog/founder-advice/why-most-pitch-decks-dont-work-and-how-to-make-sure-yours-does

### The techniques, with attribution

Deduplicated across Cicero, Conor Neill at IESE, Nancy Duarte, Chris Anderson at
TED, Carmine Gallo, Toastmasters, the MIT Communication Lab and Stanford. Every
modern list is a rediscovery of the classical exordium, and its three jobs organize
them better than any modern taxonomy. Those jobs are to make the audience
*attentus* (attentive), *docilis* (ready to learn) and *benevolus* (well disposed).

**Ordered best to worst for an INVESTOR pitch.** That ordering is not the one you
would use for a general audience, where the story open would sit first. It is built
from the unanimity of the investor-side sources below, which put identity before
any hook. Then comes the strength of the evidence behind each technique, then how
well it survives a hard time limit.

The running example is LinkedIn's Series B deck of August 2004, as annotated by Reid
Hoffman at https://www.reidhoffman.org/linkedin-pitch-to-greylock/. Every example
is an illustration written in the manner of that deck, not a line from it, and the
slide numbers are the ones printed on the slides.

| # | Opening | What it is | Example for LinkedIn, 2004 Series B | Class |
|---:|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Declarative identity, then roadmap** | Name the company and what it does in one sentence, then the shape of what follows. No setup | Illustration: LinkedIn lets you find and contact the people you need through the people you already trust. In five minutes: the network, who pays for what sits on top, and the raise (slide 1) | B, and unanimous among investor sources |
| 2 | **Extraordinary number** | The one number that makes an investor sit up, said first. It needs a comparison the audience can picture, because a number they cannot scale is a number they discard | Illustration: The Series A plan promised about 400,000 users by now. The network has 900,000, growing 29,000 a week, on less than $4 million spent (slides 11, 14 and 16) | C |
| 3 | **Relevance open** | One sentence answering the unspoken "why should I care", before anything about you | Illustration: eBay, PayPal and Google each became valuable when a network replaced a flat directory. Professional people search is still a flat directory (slides 4 to 8) | B |
| 4 | **Narrative cold open** | Start mid-scene with one specific person at one moment, the way you would begin a story told to a child | Illustration: One member contacted ten product managers through their network and hired one (slide 26). Five founder stories are on file for other decks: see "The story bank" below | **A**, but see the investor caveat |
| 5 | **Contrast: what is versus what could be** | The status quo in the audience's own language, then the gap to a better future | Illustration: Today a professional is judged by their own claims in a resume database. On a network they are judged through the people who have worked with them (slides 3 and 8) | C |
| 6 | **Bold assertion of a contested view** | Your claim plus what is at stake, one sentence, no hedging | Illustration: Where the network is the asset, revenue comes second and the first mover wins. PayPal waited until 4 million users before turning on revenue (slide 9) | C |
| 7 | **Object or image reveal** | Put one artifact, image or short clip up and let it do the work | Illustration: Open on a live profile page and its connections, and say only that this is what a member sees. The deck itself used mockups of unbuilt products for the same job (slides 23, 25 and 28) | C |
| 8 | **Personal founder insight** | The thing you know that the room does not | Illustration: The founder's insight is that PayPal's fraud detection worked only once it saw the whole network of transactions. Professional reputation has the same shape (slides 6 and 34) | C |
| 9 | **Question** | See "Starting with a question" below: the form matters and so does the audience | Illustration, cover tagline plus our question: Your network is bigger than you think. Why can nobody search it? **Not for a five-minute pitch.** Better in a short elevator pitch, where attention is the vote | A, but conditional and the conditions run against this audience |
| 10 | **Dramatic silence** | Say nothing for several seconds so the room settles onto you | Generic: hold the Problem slide and say nothing for three seconds. **Caveat:** that is one percent of a five-minute hard stop | C, single source |
| 11 | **Quotation** | A short line from someone the audience already respects, chosen because it states your thesis better than you can | None advised for this deck; see the cautions below | C |

**The trap to avoid.** A famous line used outside its real context. Franklin's
"an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure" is quoted for everything from
software to insurance, and it is about fire safety. He wrote it for the
Pennsylvania Gazette on 1735-02-04, and the proverb predates him by five
centuries in Bracton's *De Legibus*.

A room with one person who knows that is a room where the opening line has just
cost credibility instead of buying it. The general rule: if you cannot name the
publication and the year, do not use the quotation.

These were two rows until the examples showed they were not two techniques. "An
extraordinary metric" and "a surprising statistic" differ only in where the number
comes from. The second was really a delivery rule, anchor it to a comparison,
rather than a separate opening.

The sources agree: Conor Neill has one shocking factoid, and Y Combinator's "do not
bury the lead" is about ordering rather than a distinct device. So they are one
row, and the delivery rule is folded into it.

The extraordinary-number row must use a figure the deck itself can source. In the
LinkedIn example every figure comes from the annotated deck, with its slide number
beside it, so a reader can check each one in a minute. A world-scale statistic
borrowed from a third party has to meet the same bar.

### The story bank

Five public founder stories, logged so they can be reused instead of reconstructed
each time. Four are told by the founders themselves or documented by sources that
were read; the fifth is included because an insider disputes it.

**Read the Fit column first.** A story earns its place only when it shows the
mechanism the product sells. A moving story about something the product does not
do invites the obvious question of whether the product would have done it, and the
honest answer is no.

| Case | The story | The argument it makes | Fit | Status and source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Airbnb, 2007** (Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia) | Two founders could not pay their rent. A design conference had sold out the city's hotels, so they offered airbeds and breakfast in their own apartment. In October 2007 three strangers slept on their floor and paid | The founders performed the core transaction by hand before any platform existed: spare space on one side, a stranded traveler on the other, money changing hands. A two-sided need, tested at the scale of one apartment | Shows the mechanism. It does not show trust, safety or payments, which came later | Founder told; see the sources below |
| **Dropbox, founded 2007** (Drew Houston) | Houston says he boarded a bus from Boston to New York, realized he had forgotten his thumb drive, and was annoyed because it kept happening. He opened his editor on the bus and started writing code | Names the exact pain the product removes, a file stranded on a device you do not have with you, and makes the founder the first user | Entry point, not the whole pitch. Houston and the 2007 Y Combinator application frame the product more broadly, as keeping a team's files in sync and backed up | Founder told, on Houston's word alone; see the sources below |
| **Slack, 2012** (Stewart Butterfield) | The game studio announced in November 2012 that its audience was too small to survive, and shut the servers on 2012-12-09. The core team had run on IRC and was living on a Slack prototype within a month | A team turned its own working habit into the product after the original plan failed. The internal communication setup was the one thing that worked | Shows the mechanism, with one correction: the prototype was built in a month on habits formed in IRC, not a finished internal tool released as is | Documented; see the sources below |
| **Spanx, 1998 to 2000** (Sara Blakely) | In 1998 Blakely wanted a smooth line under white pants for a party and cut the feet off her control-top pantyhose. Two years of prototypes followed. The Netflix row is the teaching point. The late-fee story feels right and sells the
benefit, and an insider still contradicts it. Randolph, the co-founder and first
CEO, says the idea did not come from late fees
([WBUR On Point, 2019-11-07](https://www.wbur.org/onpoint/2019/11/07/netflix-marc-rudolph-that-will-never-work)).
A pitch opening has to survive a fact check by the people in the room, and a
co-founder is one of them.

Two honesty notes that apply to the set as a whole:

- **A story is one case, not evidence.** Say that this is what happened to one
  founder once, not that it proves the market or the model.
- **A story that a checkable source contradicts costs more than it buys.** One
  person in the room who knows the contradiction makes the opening cost
  credibility instead of earning it, and every later slide is read with more
  suspicion.

### The figure bank, and a worked example of how a number drifts

Companion to the story bank. A headline figure gets reused across a deck, an
application and a website, and once it drifts it drifts everywhere at once. The
worked example below is invented, because the mechanism is the lesson.

**What holds up.** A figure holds up when the cited page contains the cited number
and the number is scoped to what the company sells. It helps when the raw extract
behind it sits beside the document that uses it.

**A made-up case, with made-up numbers.** A deck says 40 percent of a market is
addressable and footnotes an industry report. The report says 40 percent of the
online segment is addressable, and the online segment is a quarter of the market.
The honest figure is 10 percent.

**What does not hold up:**

- The deck and the report describe different quantities, and the page behind the
  footnote does not contain the number the deck attributes to it.
- A reader who clicks the footnote finds a different statistic. That is the worst
  available failure mode in front of a diligence reader.
- The company's own appendix already carried the 10 percent, correctly scoped.
  Somewhere between two documents the word online was lost, and the number
  quadrupled.

**The drift, traced end to end.** Recalled later from memory, the figure shrank
again to a third value on a slide. So the deck and the chart disagreed, neither
number could be derived from the footnote, and the right number had been in an
internal document the whole time.

The lesson is not "check your sources". It is "check whether you already have the
answer". The failure was not research; a scoped figure moved from one document to
another and shed its scope on the way.

**The fix was not to correct the smaller number upward to the larger one.** The
larger number inherited the lost scope, so it was the weaker claim. The fix was to
put back the figure that is sourced, checkable and scoped to what the product sells.

**Keep the raw extract, not just the number.** Store the export behind the
corrected figure beside the appendix that uses it, unzipped so it is greppable and
diffable. Add a README stating what it does and does not establish. A figure whose
source data is one directory away is a figure nobody has to re-search.

**Four transferable lessons:**

1. **Scope the headline figure to what you actually sell.** A figure about the
   whole field invites the question of why you only cover part of it. A figure
   scoped to your part answers it in the same breath.
2. **A figure that cannot survive a click on its own footnote is worse than no
   figure.** Check that the cited page contains the cited number, every time.
3. **The bigger number is not the stronger one.** It is the one whose derivation
   holds.
4. **Search your own files before searching the web.** The correctly scoped figure
   already existed internally. Every hour spent re-deriving it was spent because
   nobody knew it was there, which is an argument for one evidence document per
   claim-heavy slide rather than per pitch.

### A story is owned by whoever can say "I"

Consent and deliverability are two different questions, and the second one decides
which story to use.

Chesky and Gebbia can tell the Airbnb story as "we", because they were in the
apartment. Anyone else telling it is reporting. In their mouths it is our first
three guests; in anyone else's it becomes the founders' first three guests, which
is a materially weaker thing. The speaker is now reporting rather than testifying,
and the room can hear the difference.

That matters because many pitch formats allow one presenter for both the short
elevator pitch and the longer pitch. So the presenter's own story is the only
first-person option, and every borrowed story is second-hand.

**Which to use where, given that:**

- **The presenter's own story, in both formats.** It is the only first-person
  option, it needs nobody's permission, and it should demonstrate a mechanism
  rather than a misfortune. Spanx is the model: only Blakely can say she cut the
  feet off her own pantyhose.
- **A two-founder story, when either founder presents.** Airbnb is the model:
  Chesky or Gebbia can testify to hosting three strangers in their own apartment;
  a hired presenter can only report it.
- **A co-founder's story, only when that co-founder presents.** Dropbox is the
  model: the bus and the forgotten drive are Houston's. Told second-hand it still
  works, but say plainly whose story it is rather than blurring it.
- **A documented team story, wherever the objection is that the plan might
  fail.** Slack is the model: the studio's own announcement documents the failure,
  so the pivot needs nobody's testimony and anyone can tell it.
- **A disputed story: the appendix at most, and better nowhere.** Netflix is the
  model: an insider contradicts the mechanism, so it cannot open a pitch. If it
  comes up from the floor, name the dispute.

**Rows 4 and 9 are inverted on purpose.** The story open has the best experimental
support of any technique and ranks fourth here. The question open also has genuine
experimental support and ranks ninth.

Both are demoted for the same reason, which is the audience. The investor-side
sources are unanimous that identity comes before any hook, and the
rhetorical-question effect reverses against a high-involvement audience. Neither
is a weak technique; they are strong techniques aimed at a different room.

**The story open is the only one with direct experimental support for the opening
position specifically** (Andeweg, de Jong and Hoeken, *Technical Communication
Quarterly* 7(3), 1998). It is also the only technique recommended by every
independent source surveyed, and it tops the one catalog that counted. Andeweg and
de Jong went through 136 twentieth-century Dutch-language speaking manuals and
narrowed them to 42 with expert help. The anecdote was the most recommended
attention-getter.

One honest caveat on the whole table. Van De Mieroop, de Jong and Andeweg analyzed
the introductions of 40 engineering speeches (*Journal of Business and Technical
Communication*, 2008). Real expert speakers mostly do not use the technique the
manuals recommend most. Either the advice is wrong or the field
ignores good advice, and nobody has established which.

### Starting with a question

This technique needs its condition attached. It is one of the few presentation
techniques with a real experimental literature, and that literature is
conditional.

Conor Neill of IESE Business School teaches it as one of his three main openings,
and ranks it third, behind the story and the shocking factoid. He defines it as
phrasing the problem the audience already lives with as a question they cannot
answer comfortably.
([IESE](https://www.iese.edu/standout/how-to-start-speech/))

**Three forms, and the experiments found different results for each. Do not
conflate them.**

1. **Rhetorical question.** No answer expected; the only sensible answer supports
   you. This is the measured form.
2. **Hypophora.** You ask, then answer it yourself within a sentence or two, so it
   works as a curiosity gap rather than a request for agreement. Zillmann and
   Cantor (*British Journal of Educational Psychology*, 1973) found improved recall
   of the facts presented, chiefly when attention was otherwise low.
3. **Prequestion.** You ask the question your talk will answer, then answer it over
   the talk. Reliably improves learning of the specific information the question
   addressed, less reliably anything else, with no detriment found.
4. **Genuine show of hands.** Practitioner advice only. No experiment tests it as
   an opening.

**The condition that matters: a question only helps an audience that was not
already thinking hard.**

A rhetorical question works by making people think harder about what you just
said. That is worth something when they were half paying attention. It is worth
nothing when they were already concentrating, and it can actively get in the way.

Petty, Cacioppo and Heesacker (*JPSP* 40(3), 1981) tested exactly that. They gave
people an argument, phrased either as plain statements or as rhetorical questions,
and varied how much the topic personally affected them. When the topic barely
affected them, the questions made them engage more, so a strong argument landed
better and a weak one landed worse. When the topic affected them a great deal, they
described the questions as distracting, and they engaged with the argument less
than the people who got plain statements.

An investor deciding whether to give you money is the second case. They are already
paying as much attention as they have. A question cannot raise attention that is
already at the ceiling, so the only thing it can do is spend some of it.

Two things partly rescue it. Burnkrant and Howard (*JPSP* 47(6), 1984) reran the
same message with the questions moved to the introduction rather than after each
argument, and in print rather than spoken. They reported enhanced elaboration
regardless of relevance. So an introductory question in a document the reader
controls is a different and safer proposition than a spoken rhetorical question
mid-argument.

Treat that as Petty and Cacioppo's reading of the study rather than verified from
the primary text.

**Where it backfires:**

- **Once the audience notices the question as a device.** Ahluwalia and Burnkrant
  (*JCR* 31(1), 2004) show that high salience of the rhetorical form redirects
  elaboration away from your argument and onto you as a persuasion agent.
- **In front of a weak argument.** Every experiment crossing question form with
  argument quality found the same asymmetry: raising elaboration amplifies
  whatever comes next, in both directions. A question opening is a bet on the
  strength of what follows.
- **Repeated through the talk.** Munch and Swasy (1988) found rhetoricals reduced
  argument recall, worse with frequency.
- **Delivered with rhetorical intonation.** The only experiment set in a sales
  pitch had 72 listeners rate speakers, including on the probability of investing
  (Neitsch and Niebuhr, *Journal of Psycholinguistic Research* 51(5), 2022).
  Delivery dominated wording, and rhetorical-question prosody lowered speaker
  ratings.
- **As a show of hands with a small, senior or unfamiliar audience**, where a
  non-response is the likely outcome and there is no recovery.
- **In writing, on a title slide or a subject line**, where the reader is still
  deciding whether to engage at all. Question framing measurably hurts there,
  across 53,030 Reddit posts, more than three million academic articles, 22,743
  headline A/B tests and a preregistered experiment.

Honest limit: no experiment in this literature used investors, funding decisions or
a live pitch. The high-involvement condition was undergraduates facing a graduation
requirement.

### The investor audience is different, and the sources are unanimous

This is where general presentation coaching and investor-side advice pull apart,
and for a funding pitch the investor side wins.

Every named investor-side source says open by stating plainly what the company
does, with no preamble and no problem setup:

- Michael Seibel and Kevin Hale at Y Combinator ("no need to set up the problem,
  you can just get to the point"), and Geoff Ralston on demo day.
- Sequoia's own template ("define your company in a single declarative sentence").
- Reid Hoffman's annotated LinkedIn Series B deck: "I now believe you should begin
  and end with the investment thesis"
  ([reidhoffman.org](https://www.reidhoffman.org/linkedin-pitch-to-greylock/)).

The one accelerator that does advocate a hook, Techstars via Nicole Glaros, names
five devices, and a question is not among them.

The research explains why rather than leaving it as a style preference:

- **Experts want concrete "how", novices want abstract "why".** Falchetti, Cattani
  and Ferriani (*Strategic Management Journal*, 2022, four experiments) found that
  lay evaluators and crowdfunders respond to abstract framing. Expert evaluators
  respond to concrete framing of how the thing works.
- **Investors fund perceived preparedness, not displayed passion.** Chen, Yao and
  Kotha (*AMJ*, 2009), lab experiment plus a field study of real business-plan
  competitions.
- **The mechanism is cognitive legitimacy**, meaning the evaluator understands what
  the business is. Pollack, Rutherford and Nagy (*ETP*, 2012) coded 113 funded
  pitches across Shark Tank and Dragons' Den.
- **Expert evaluators categorize you in the first seconds and weight negative cues
    heavily**, and pitchers do not recover. Elsbach and Kramer (*AMJ*, 2003) studied
  28 Hollywood pitch meetings in the field; the cues that sank a pitch included
  seeming too slick, formulaic, or pitching by rote. Conversely, openings that got
  the evaluator contributing their own ideas raised their judgment of the pitcher.
- **In a short competition format, delivery outruns wording.** Tsay (*Academy of
  Management Discoveries*, 2021, 12 studies, 1,855 participants) tested 60-second
  pitches. Six seconds of silent video from the start predicted the investors'
  actual picks better than the audio did.
- **Your slot in the running order matters and your opening cannot fix it.**
  Clingingsmith, Conley and Shane (*Journal of Innovation Economics and
  Management*, 2022) randomized position across four university elevator-pitch
  competitions: pitching early scored lower.

### What to avoid

- **Preamble**: thanks, self-introduction, agenda, credentials. Every authority says
  no, and it remains the most common opening in real professional practice.
- **A joke.** Most attempted jokes in a professional talk do not land, and a failed
  one is not neutral: it signals lower competence and costs status. Borrowed humor
  is worse, because the same words carry differently out of a different mouth.
- **A statistic with no comparison.** A number the audience cannot scale is a
  number they discard.
- **Story, problem setup or category-shift framing before the audience can name
  your business.** Hook first is general advice; for investors, identity first.

### Then, immediately

The opening should still lead rapidly to:

> What the company does and why it matters.
