# Failure modes

What actually goes wrong in decks, and what to delete on sight.

## Common Pitch-Deck Failure Modes

### Content failures

- Unclear company definition
- Weak problem
- No “why now”
- Top-down TAM only
- Vanity traction
- No business model
- No GTM
- “No competitors”
- Unrealistic financials
- Generic team bios
- No fundraising ask
- Ask not connected to milestones

### Narrative failures

- Founder history before company purpose
- Market before problem
- Product details before the audience knows why it matters
- Repeating the same claim on multiple slides
- Hiding the strongest evidence late in the deck
- Treating every slide as equally important

### Design failures

- Tiny type
- Full paragraphs
- Too many colors
- Decorative icons
- Low contrast
- Inconsistent spacing
- Small screenshots
- Dense legends
- 3D charts
- Excessive animations
- Multiple unrelated visual styles

### Investor-process failures

- Only one deck for every context
- No appendix
- No short pitch version
- Unable to answer interruptions
- Metrics differ between slides
- The **forwardable** deck is not understandable without narration. An emailed
  PDF carries no narration, so the argument has to be on the page or in the
  appendix. This is not a failure in a live meeting deck, where the argument
  belongs in the speaker notes by design.
- Founder does not know key numbers

## Things to Remove Aggressively

Remove unless they earn their place:

- “Thank You” slide with no useful content
- Generic stock imagery
- Multiple mission statements
- Feature lists
- Empty buzzwords
- Unnecessary logos
- Multiple graphs proving the same thing
- Decorative gradients
- Clip art
- Long quotes
- Overly detailed architecture in the core deck
- Full financial statements in the core deck
- 20-item roadmaps
- Detailed hiring tables in the core deck

Replace the final “Thank You” page with something valuable:

- Vision + contact
- Raise + milestones
- Summary of strongest proof points

### The removal test

Before you keep an element, ask four questions:

1. Does it carry information?
2. Does it establish hierarchy?
3. Does it support brand?
4. Does it improve comprehension?

One qualification on question 3: brand support is not on its own a reason to keep
a decorative element. A stripe, an accent bar or a colored edge that only says
“this is our brand” fails the test, because the enforced layer removes those
unconditionally. Brand support counts only for an element that also passes one of
the other three questions.

### Also remove

- **Accent lines under titles.**
- **Decorative color bars and edge stripes**, on slides and on cards alike.
- **Extra shades of your own brand color.** Three tints of one hue is the
  ceiling; nine reads as indecision. See `05-color-and-access.md`.

### Source lines are kept, not removed

Keep the source line on the slide, set at the floor for the mode: 18 pt on a
live deck, 14 pt on a read-alone deck. Only the overflow provenance moves to the
appendix. Never shrink the line below the floor to make it fit; shorten the text
instead.

Drop the on-slide source line only for a **live projected** deck, where you say
the provenance out loud. On a forwardable deck the source line is the only
provenance the reader ever gets, so it stays.

## General Business-Presentation Structure

For a non-investor business presentation, use the narrative architecture in
`01-narrative.md` and change three things.

1. The deck ends in a decision, not in a fundraising ask: state the
   recommendation, then who does what by when.
2. Market size, team and traction sections usually drop out. What replaces them
   is the evidence and the implication: why the audience should believe the
   claim, and what follows from it if they do.
3. The opening is context rather than company purpose, because the audience
   already knows who you are.

That gives a working sequence of context, question, claim, evidence,
implication, recommendation, action.

MIT’s single-point and assertion-title guidance applies unchanged outside
startup fundraising.
